Community Safety Indicators (C.S.I.)
What are Community Safety Indicators (C.S.I.)?

Community Safety Indicators (C.S.I.) is a term used by the Toronto Police Service to present police-reported information that reflects community safety.

C.S.I. replaces the term Major Crime Indicators (M.C.I.) and reflects a shift toward clearer, more inclusive, and community-focused language — one that goes beyond “major crime” and includes other incidents that have a significant impact on communities.

What indicators are included in Community Safety Indicators (C.S.I.)?

Community Safety Indicators (C.S.I.) published on the Public Safety Data Portal (P.S.D.P.) currently include:

  • Assault
  • Auto Theft
  • Break and Enter
  • Robbery
  • Theft Over
  • Homicide
  • Shootings and Firearm Discharges
  • Traffic Fatalities (Dashboard format only)
  • Hate Crimes
  • Intimate Partner and Family Violence

These indicators are all part of the Community Safety Indicators (C.S.I.) framework, even though they may be published across multiple datasets.

Why are some Community Safety Indicators (C.S.I.) published in separate datasets?

Not all Community Safety Indicators (C.S.I.) are combined into a single dataset.

The Community Safety Indicators (C.S.I.) open dataset currently mirrors the former Major Crime Indicators (M.C.I.) dataset and includes:

  • Assault
  • Auto Theft
  • Break and Enter
  • Robbery
  • Theft Over

Other Community Safety Indicators (C.S.I.)—Homicide, Shootings and Firearm Discharges, Hate Crimes, Intimate Partner and Family Violence (I.P.V.), and Traffic Fatalities (Dashboard format only) —are published as separate datasets or dashboards due to differences in reporting requirements, definitions, or analytical focus.

Together, these datasets form the broader C.S.I. framework.

How does C.S.I. relate to previously published Major Crime Indicators (M.C.I.)?

Community Safety Indicators (C.S.I.) replace the term Major Crime Indicators (M.C.I.) across the Public Safety Data Portal (P.S.D.P.) and related products.

All crime categories previously included under M.C.I. (Assault, Auto Theft, Break & Enter, Robbery, and Theft Over) remain valid and comparable. No crime categories have been reclassified, removed, or added as part of this renaming.

As a result, historical data remains consistent, and trend analyses using prior M.C.I. data can continue without adjustment.

In short, Community Safety Indicators (C.S.I.) is a naming and framing update, not a change to how crimes are counted or categorized.

Will additional indicators be added to Community Safety Indicators (C.S.I.) in the future?

Yes. The Community Safety Indicators (C.S.I.) framework is designed to expand and evolve over time.

Additional datasets and indicators may be incorporated as reporting practices, data availability, and community needs change.

Auto Theft Statistics Update
What has updated in auto theft reporting?

Enhancements have been applied to the auto theft reporting logic to improve accuracy, consistency, and clarity. These enhancements help ensure reported statistics better reflect auto theft activity within the city.

What specific updates were made to the auto theft reporting logic?

The reporting logic has been refined in the following ways:

  • Expanded inclusion of motorcycles and similar vehicle types
  • Improved geographic alignment of reported incidents
  • More consistent identification and counting of stolen vehicles

Together, these refinements improve consistency and strengthen data quality across auto theft statistics.

How do these updates affect auto theft numbers?

Following these updates, auto theft numbers published in current releases may differ from figures reported in earlier publications or archived reports. The updated reporting logic has been applied consistently to historical data dating back to 2014.

For this reason, year-over-year comparisons should be made using data from the same release framework. Earlier releases may reflect different counting rules and should be compared with caution.

Do these updates affect historical data or long-term trend analysis?

Historical data remains available; however, differences in reporting approaches mean that direct comparisons with earlier releases should be interpreted with caution.

The updated logic provides a more consistent baseline moving forward, supporting clearer long-term trend analysis over time.

Intimate Partner Violence (I.P.V.)
What is Intimate Partner Violence?

The Toronto Police Service Intimate Partner Violence procedure, 05-04 defines Intimate Partner Violence. Intimate Partner Violence means any physical, sexual or psychological harm caused, or attempted, between persons involved in an intimate relationship including:

  • assault;
  • murder;
  • sexual assault;
  • threatening;
  • harassment;
  • intimidation;
  • unlawful interference with personal liberty;
  • any other criminal offence;
  • offences under other statutes, such as the Family Law Act, Children’s Law Reform Act, etc.;

       but does not include child abuse investigations.

How is the relationship between the accused and victim categorized in the Intimate Partner Violence dataset?

The 3 categories for Intimate Partner Violence are Intimate Partner, Family, and Unclassified.

Intimate Partner is defined as: an Intimate Relationship as "marriage, domestic partnership, engagement, casual or serious romantic involvement, and dating, whether in a current or former relationship. Intimate Partner Violence can occur between persons of any sex, sexual orientation, gender, gender identity, or gender expression, and it can occur in any type of intimate relationship including monogamous, non-committed, and relationships involving more than two partners.

Family is defined as: Familial relationships such as parents, siblings, or any other family members.

Unclassified is defined as: The relationship between the victim and the accused in the occurrence is not identified or indicated but the investigating officers have identified this as an intimate partner related incident.

What is a Historical Occurrence?

Historical Occurrence refers to occurrences where there is a difference between the reported date and the occurrence date by 365 days or more.

What does the Count field in the dataset represent?

The count field found in the intimate Partner Violence dataset is an aggregated count of Intimate Partner Violence data that is grouped by all preceding fields.

How can you support someone experiencing Intimate Partner Violence?

Abuse victims face many barriers including fear of further harm or injury, self-blame, shame, restrictions on finances, resources and supports. They can experience social pressures to keep their family together and other cultural taboos. You can help someone experiencing Intimate Partner Violence by:

  • empowering them to seek safety options
  • helping them to identify resources, services and options available to them
  • letting them know it is not their fault

By providing non-judgemental support, you create an opportunity for them to seek your support again.

TPS is addressing Intimate Partner Violence through building and maintaining partnerships with community organizations to promote awareness about the issue of Intimate Partner Violence and increase the awareness of available resources and support services.

In addition, TPS members are trained on IPV response, all IPV investigations are case-managed by investigators who are accredited in IPV investigations and who use a Trauma-Informed Approach that enhances victim safety and ensures a victim-focused response.

Hate Crimes
What is a hate crime?

A hate crime is a criminal offence committed against a person or property motivated in whole or in part by bias, prejudice or hate based towards an “identifiable group” based on race, national or ethnic origin, language, colour, religion, sex, age, mental or physical disability, sexual orientation or gender identity or expression or any other similar factor.

A hate crime or hate-motivated incident involves the intentional selection of a victim based on the offender’s hate/bias/prejudice towards an identifiable group.

Section 718.2(a)(i) of the Criminal Code of Canada (Criminal Code) requires a court that imposes a sentence (for any offence) to consider, “evidence that the offence was motivated by bias, prejudice or hate based on race, national or ethnic origin, language, colour, religion, sex, age, mental or physical disability, sexual orientation, or gender identity or expression, or on any other similar factor”, as an aggravating factor in deciding on the appropriate sentence. For example, if an individual carves neo-Nazi hate symbols, imagery or words such as the letters KKK and/or racial slurs, into the door of a vehicle, the offender may be charged by police with the criminal offence of mischief. The investigators will document the occurrence and collect all related evidence such as witness/victim statements, photographs and video footage. The occurrence would be classified as a hate crime. The consent of the Attorney General would not be required to commence criminal proceedings for such a case and all hate-related evidence would be presented to the courts at the time of sentencing.

What is a hate incident?

A hate incident is a non-criminal action or behaviour that is motivated by hate against an identifiable group. Examples of hate incidents include using racial slurs or insulting a person because of their ethnic or religious dress or how they identify.

A hate crime or hate-motivated incident involves the intentional selection of a victim based on the offender’s hate/bias/prejudice towards an identifiable group.

What is the difference between a hate crime and hate incident?

The difference between a hate crime and hate incident is the element of criminality. The Toronto Police Service recognizes that hate-related incidents, whether criminal or not, can have a significant impact on victims, communities and society as a whole. If unchecked, these crimes can result in escalating social tensions between different groups that can lead to isolation, stress and divisiveness.

Where does hate crime data come from?

TPS hate crime data is derived from the Hate Crime Tracker (form). This form is completed for all reported hate-related occurrences. The TPS Hate Crime Unit is responsible for the review and classification of these occurrences. The data used in the hate crimes open data and dashboard includes only verified hate crimes.

Why does TPS track hate crime data?

The Ontario Policing Standards Manual (2000) requires police services to maintain statistical data on hate-related occurrences for reports to the police service board and to the Canadian Centre for Justice Statistics (CCJS).

Hate crime trends and patterns across the City help inform TPS-wide strategies such as hate crime prevention, community outreach and public education.

The TPS Hate Crime Statistical Report is presented to the Toronto Police Service Board annually and is publicly available on the Dashboard Resource page. The Report provides important information to the public including:

  • Statistical data on reported hate crime in Toronto
  • The methodology used to classify occurrences
  • The community impact of hate crimes
  • Criminal Code – hate provisions
  • Types of offences committed
  • Education and community outreach initiatives

What is the role of the Ministry of Attorney General’s Hate Crime Working Group and how do they work with police?

Formed in 2019, the Criminal Law Division’s (CLD) Hate Crime Working Group (HCWG) is comprised of specially-trained counsel from the Crown Law Office – Criminal and representatives from Crown Attorney’s offices in each region across the province. HCWG members provide expertise and advice to police and Crowns in their regions in relation to any hate crime investigation and prosecution, and play an important role in tracking hate crime prosecutions in the province.

The HCWG encourages police to reach out as needed through identified regional representatives.

The HCWG is also available to assist anytime the police are considering laying a hate propaganda charge requiring Attorney General (AG) consent (i.e. wilful promotion of hatred, wilful promotion of antisemitism and advocating genocide), as well as where police are seeking AG consent to apply for the seizure and forfeiture of hate-related materials.

How does the law address hate propaganda offences? When is Attorney General consent required?

Hate Propaganda Sections of the Criminal Code

There are four hate propaganda offences.

“Hate propaganda” is defined in section 320(8) of the Criminal Code as: “any writing, sign or visible representation that advocates or promotes genocide or the communication of which by any person would constitute an offence under section 319”.

The hate propaganda sections of the Criminal Code list specific offences including advocating genocide (section 318), public incitement of hatred (section 319(1)), wilful promotion of hatred (section 319(2)) and wilful promotion of antisemitism (section 319 (2.1)).

Advocating Genocide – s. 318(1) – Advocating or promoting genocide against an “identifiable group”

  • “Genocide” is defined in s. 318(2)
  • “Identifiable group” is defined in s. 318(4). This definition, which applies to all four hate propaganda offences where it is mentioned, means “any section of the public distinguished by colour, race, religion, national or ethnic origin, age, sex, sexual orientation, gender identity or expression, or mental or physical disability.”
  • See Mugesera v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 2005 SCC 40 (SCC)
  • Attorney General must consent to prosecution - s. 318(3)

Public Incitement of Hatred – s. 319(1) – By communicating statements in any public place, incites hatred against any identifiable group where such incitement is likely to lead to a breach of the peace
  • “Communicating,” “public place” and “statements” are defined in s. 319(7). Note that “statements” includes “words spoken or written or recorded electronically or electromagnetically or otherwise, and gestures, signs or other visible representations”
  • Attorney General consent is not required

Wilful Promotion of Hatred – s. 319(2) – By communicating statements, other than in private conversation, wilfully promotes hatred against any identifiable group
  • The meaning of “wilful,” “promotes,” and “hatred” are addressed in R. v. Keegstra, [1990] 3 S.C.R. 697 (SCC). “Hatred” connotes an emotion of an intense and extreme nature that is clearly associated with vilification and detestation. Only the most intense forms of dislike are covered
  • Attorney General must consent to prosecution (s. 319(6))
  • See R. v. Krymowski [2005] 1 S.C.R. 101, 2005 (SCC) re issues of proof respecting targeting of Roma community
  • See also Warman v. Kouba [2006]CHRT No. 50 which provides a useful list of 11 “Hallmarks of Hate” which, although neither mandatory nor exhaustive, provide a helpful indication of the kinds of communications that cross the line from lawful speech to hate speech

Wilful Promotion of Antisemitism – s. 319(2.1): By communicating statements, other than in private conversation, that wilfully promotes antisemitism by condoning, denying or downplaying the Holocaust
  • New provision – enacted in June 2022
  • This provision is strictly limited to statements aimed at condoning, denying, or downplaying the Holocaust. Hateful communications targeting members of the Jewish community are prosecuted under one of the other propaganda offences (i.e., wilful promotion of hatred, public incitement of hatred, or advocating genocide). Any combination of Criminal Code offences can be laid depending on the facts of the case
  • Attorney General must consent to prosecution s. 319(6)

What is the difference between race and ethnicity?

Race and ethnicity are two distinct terms referring to either physical or cultural characteristics of groups of people. Race can be understood as a system of social classification. Canada’s Anti-Racism Strategy 2019–2022 clarifies that, "race is a ‘social construct.’ This means that society forms ideas of race based on geographic, historical, political, economic, social and cultural factors, as well as physical traits.” In alignment with Ontario's Anti-Racism Data Standards, TPS uses the following broad race categories: Black, White, Middle Eastern, South Asian, Latino, Indigenous, and East/Southeast Asian.

Ethnicity refers to the cultural characteristics of people from a certain geographic region. This can include factors such as language, religion, and customs. The Ontario Human Rights Commission describes ethnicity as "groups sharing a distinctive cultural and historical tradition often associated with race, place of origin, ancestry or creed."

What is the difference between sexual orientation and gender?

Sexual orientation refers to how a person describes their sexuality. It encompasses a broad range of identities; for example, someone may describe themselves as hetrosexual, gay, lesbian, or bisexual. Sexual orientation is closely related to a person's sexual attraction and behaviour but is distinct from gender and the sex assigned at birth. Statistics Canada explains that "sexual orientation is a complex and evolving subject for which there is no universally accepted definition. How people define their sexuality and the words that they use to do so are affected by ongoing social change, making sexual orientation a difficult concept to define." TPS uses the broad categories of asexual, bisexual, gay, straight/hetrosexual, lesbian, pansexual, questioning, and "other sexual orientation".

Gender refers to an individual's personal and social identity as a woman, man, or non-binary person. Gender is closely related to identity and expression. Gender identity refers to how an individual feels personally and individually. Gender expression refers to how an individual presents themselves (i.e., through factors such as accessories, body language, and aesthetic choices) regardless of their gender identity. The gender of a person does not always match their assigned sex at birth and may differ from current legal identifications or documentation. TPS uses the broad categories of female, male, gender diverse (non-binary), transgender, two-spirit, and other.

Why are the arrest rates so low for hate crimes?

It is often very difficult to identify suspects due to the lack of witnesses, digital and/or forensic evidence. Moreover, many hate crimes occur without the victim present, as in the case of hate motivated graffiti or mischief.

What is a “bias category”?

Hate crime occurrences are classified using categories based on the criteria listed in s. 718.2(a)(i) of the Criminal Code: “race, national or ethnic origin, language, colour, religion, sex, age, mental or physical disability, sexual orientation, or gender, or other similar factor”. These categories are referred to as “bias category”.

Why do the number of hate crime occurrences and the identifiable groups targeted fluctuate throughout the year?

Hate crime trends may differ throughout the year for many different reasons. Geopolitical events (wars, politics, notable events, etc.) are typically the driving factor for changes in hate crime trends. Education, training, increased media coverage, etc., can also contribute to the fluctuation of the number of reported hate crimes and the particular community groups victimized.

Why do the total number of reported hate crimes change when adding multiple bias category?

Some hate crime occurrences have more than one bias category, when selecting multiple bias categories simultaneously this will only represent the occurrences where both biases were present in the same occurrence.

What is “Other similar factor” in s. 718.2(a)(i)?

“Other similar factor” includes factors not innumerate specifically in section 718.2 of the Criminal Code, but for which the police service believes that the individual was targeted with hatred/bias/prejudice because of an identifiable characteristic. This could include, for example, hatred of the victim’s profession, immigrant/refugee status, or their socio-economic status (e.g. individuals who are unhoused, etc.).

Analytics
What is the difference between year-to-date and year end?

Year-to-Date (YTD):refers to the period beginning on January 1st of the current year up to and including the present date or date as indicated. The same time period may be applied across multiple years in order to determine trends over time.

Year End:refers to the full year period beginning on January 1st and ending on December 31st. This time period may be applied across multiple years in order to compare year over year changes and/or determine trends over time.

Why is the data count presented in the Crime App YTD different from the YTD Data Analytics Dashboards for C.S.I., Homicide and Shootings & Firearm Discharges?

The Crime App YTD is updated daily and displays data accurate as of the previous day while the data analytics dashboards for C.S.I., Homicides and Shootings & Firearm Discharges are updated on the first business day of the week and are accurate as of Sunday at 11:59PM of the current week.

How often are the Data Analytics Dashboards updated?

YTD Dashboards are updated weekly on the first business day of the week and are accurate as of Sunday at 11:59PM of the current week.

Historical Dashboards are updated quarterly, aligned with each open data release.

Why does the historical data presented in the YTD dashboards change over time?

All YTD dashboards display data for the same time period from January 1 to the most recent date of each year. For example, on June 30th, the data for each year will be from January 1 to June 30th for 2016, 2017, 2018, etc. respectively. If you're interested in a snapshot of the year end totals for the historical years, please view the Year End dashboards.
